🎮 How you give the command

Every recipe in this module is triggered the same way: in Claude Code, you type reel edits followed by the path to your vertical video. Then you have two ways:

1. Let the skill propose

Run it solo reel edits ~/videos/bruto.mp4. The skill reads the video and suggests 2–3 treatments with a recommendation—you choose which one to follow.

2. Natural language guide

Write the intention after the path, separated by a —. That's exactly what each recipe below does — each one includes a command ready to copy.

The pure approach 1—she reads it and gives you the options:

you type in Claude Code

Either way, it always shows the clean cut for you to approve before animating; then it assembles everything and runs the reviewer until it gets PASS. They’re equivalent to reel edits: make a reel from this video: <path> or /reel-<your-brand> <path>.

1

🎬 Pro Editor: full screen + B-roll

The most common recipe and the foundation of almost every reel: you in full screen speaking, with Real B-roll coming in on top at the right moments. It’s the default treatment — when you run the skill without asking for anything special, this is where it starts.

New here? — “overlay” and “B-roll”

Overlay is a layer that goes over the main image (you) without replacing it entirely. B-roll is the supporting clip/image that illustrates what you’re saying. In Editor Pro, B-roll is an overlay that appears and disappears as the speech calls for it.

In practice, it’s what runs by default. Point the skill to your raw video:

Objective: generate the reel using the base recipe (Editor Pro) 
reel edita <caminho-do-seu-video.mp4>

How to verify: the reel comes out with you full-screen and B-roll appearing at the right moments. If you want a different recipe for a segment, just ask (e.g., "use screenshare for the demo").

What it is

You full screen with real B-roll overlaid—the default recipe.

Why learn

It’s the one you’ll use most; knowing when B-roll comes in keeps your reel from feeling monotonous.

Key concepts

Full screen, B-roll overlay, base recipe.

2

🖼️ B-roll PiP: Picture-in-Picture

In the picture-in-picture (PiP), you stay on screen: you remain visible while the B-roll appears in a smaller window, at the same time. Your face and the supporting footage share the frame — unlike the Pro Editor, where B-roll covers you briefly.

💡 When to choose PiP instead of an overlay

Use PiP when your reaction matters as much as the supporting material — reacting to a comment, commenting on a screenshot, showing something, and looking at the camera at the same time. If the supporting material needs the whole screen to be readable, use the overlay (Editor Pro) or screenshare instead.

🖼️ PiP (Picture-in-Picture)

You and the B-roll appear together. Good for reactions, commentary, and “look at this while I talk.”

🎬 Overlay (Editor Pro)

The B-roll covers you for a few seconds, then you’re back. Good when the supporting footage needs the spotlight.

Why learn: choosing between PiP and overlay is what gives the reel intention — it’s not decoration; it’s about what the viewer needs to see in that second.

Key concepts: PiP, floating window, face and supporting footage together.

3

🖥️ Screenshare + Demo: Face Moves Down and Shares the Screen

The tutorial and demo recipe: you start full-screen, and when it’s time to show something, your face shrinks to a small PiP while the shared screen takes over the frame. When the demo ends, you get back to growing.

1

You present (full screen)

Introduce what you’re about to show, looking at the camera. The viewer connects with you first.

2

Face drops → screen takes over

During the demo, you appear as a PiP in the corner while the shared screen fills the frame. You can see what you’re doing without losing sight of you.

3

Back to full screen

Once the demonstration is over, you grow back to close the point. The transition adds dynamism.

Why learn: is the only recipe that shows “how to do it” in practice without turning into a soulless screencast—the face in PiP keeps the human presence. Key concepts: screenshare, face→PiP transition, demo.

4

▚ Split screen: two sides, one screen

In the split, the screen splits into two halves that appear at the same time: you on one side, the supporting footage on the other — or before × after, option A × option B. It's the comparison recipe: the contrast is obvious because the two are side by side.

Where the split shines

Before × after an edit, code × result, “the hard way × the easy way.” Whenever the content is a comparison, split-screen delivers the message without you needing to explain it in words.

Why learn: comparison is one of the formats that keeps viewers watching most; the split makes it instant. Key concepts: split, comparison, visual contrast.

Objective: request the split-screen recipe (split).

5

💥 Moving Text with Punch

This isn’t a caption—it’s motion graphics. The keyword comes in with punch (scale, weight, a sharp SFX) at the exact moment you say it. It anchors the eye on what matters and creates the "visual punch" that holds attention within the ≤4s rhythm.

✓ Punch with purpose

  • ✓Only the word that carries the point
  • ✓Comes in synchronized with the speech and an SFX
  • ✓It gets out before it becomes noise

✗ Generic captions (avoid)

  • ✗Every spoken line transcribed in full
  • ✗Text that repeats what's already being said
  • ✗The same style throughout everything (AI look)

Why learn: the punch is what turns a "video with captions" into a "video edited with intention." Key concepts: motion graphics, punch, highlighted word.

6

🎞️ Real elements: actual captures and clips

Instead of a generic stock image, this recipe inserts the real element than what you reference: the repository capture, the website screenshot, the product clip — often inside a browser frame. It's the difference between "talking about something" and "showing the thing."

🔑 Real > generic, always

Just any “code” icon proves nothing. A real capture of your repository, with its name and stars visible, is proof—and proof sticks. The engine prefers the real thing and uses a generic image only when there’s no source.

What it is

Real capture/clip of what you mention, in a browser frame.

Why learn

Credibility: the real thing is more convincing than any generic image.

Key concepts

Real capture, browser frame, proof.

7

❄️ Cold open: 0–3s of impact

The required opening for every reel. The first 0–3 seconds need to grab attention before of any introduction — showing the real result, a hook strong or a fun fact. No “hi, today I'm going to talk about...”: that loses the viewer in the first second.

Actual result

Show the “after” right away—the finished reel, the final number—and only then explain how you got there.

Hook

A punchy line or question that opens a loop in the viewer’s mind.

Fun fact

An unexpected number that makes you want to know the story behind it.

Why learn: the cold open is where the reel lives or dies — it’s the recipe that determines retention. Key concepts: cold open, hook, opening hook.
